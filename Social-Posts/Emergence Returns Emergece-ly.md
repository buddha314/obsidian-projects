Hey party people! Remember Chaos Theory? Remember when Jurassic Park introduced "Chaotitian" to the world? Spicy! Adjacent to Chaos Theory back in the 90s was Complexity Theory, championed by the geniuses at the Santa Fe Institute. I got into Complexity Theory via John Holland's "Adaption and Natural Selection..." + word count.

The hook was Emergence. How large numbers of self-guided agents could create group behaviors that could not be explained by individual actions. The most compelling example is the brain, but I still stand in my yard and stare at bees for long enough to concern my neighbors.  The math behind those systems is intrinsically interesting and complex. Turns out it's a bit too complex for use in industrial / enterprise applications.  I did get to use Lotka-Volterra once for a pricing model. That was awesome.

Anything resembling Differential Game Theory (Rufus Isaacs) was both too complex to explain to executives and, frankly, really expensive to run as an active system. So we all moved to more practical families. If you did any kind of enterprise application after 1996, you were doing Support Vector Machines.

But on the the point! With the rise of #syntheticbiology and #quantumcomputing I see those methods returning with a vengeance. I'm kinda giddy about it.  I believe future advances in therapeutics will be pulled FROM the market by listening to patient voices, not pushed TO the market by the major pharma companies. The majors have hit a wall in drug discovery and precision medicine is going to make that wall higher. I think I'll talk about that again.

Thirty years ago, drug discovery was about at most a few hundred biomarkers / diagnostics. Now that you can get a full genome sequence for like $600, we are going to be dealing with hundreds of thousands, if not millions of biomarkers. GLORIOUS!  I want to see swarms of interacting metabolites, exozymes, cell-cell-interactions (nods to Dino DiCarlo) and all those non-linear interaction agents of chaos that make this world so fabulously interesting. 

The rise of LLMs has re-kindled this field, I think. It's raised the expectation of AI in general, and users are looking for syllogistic logic and dynamical systems predictions. But these poor suckers are just word predictors. If we're getting back to interacting systems, like weather systems or proteomics, we're gonna need more than linear algebra.

Who else is ready to bust out Octave again and do some PDEs? Do they have an Ito module for Octave now? 

In researching for this post, I learned that the term "Complexity Theory" has sort of been abandoned, and now mostly concerns itself with Computational Complexity. Not as fun.

Many of these ideas, of course, were inspired by listening to hours and hours of the Grow Everything podcast.
