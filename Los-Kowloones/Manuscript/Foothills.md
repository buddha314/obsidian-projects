Seven years later.

Harhal was alone for the first time in over thirty hours. It was 1 AM and he was on the shore of a vast asphalt lake that served as the Los Kowloones Mall parking lot. A fence larger than he'd ever seen enclosed him in a mishegoss of plywood, black tarp and chainlink. The fence disappeared into the night in two directions.   A fierce, chaotic wind was causing the fence to thump like irregular beats of high schooler tuning a timpani. The thin door to the guard trailer offered a weak companion snare. Opening, hesitating, smashing, opening again and looking around to see what the other doors were doing.

When the wind came, it came in gusts that felt like a warm hug, and took the breath from your mouth like a gasp of terror. He smelled a new kind of desert on the wind. He smelled a vast, hidden city. He smelled a tremendous fire.

The urgency in his body echoed in the landscape about him. Sirens blared from indistinguishable directions, their panic cutting the wind. Volcanic plumes of fire spewed to the "that way". He had no idea how far he was from the flames. He had no idea what had started the conflagration. He no idea what he was supposed to do. He was out of ideas.

Following the direction of the woman on the phone, he had stepped outside of the trailer to "check things out." He grabbed his new SECURITY jacket, belt, radio and baton, pausing before he picked up the new gun.

The trailer was near the corner of the property and the hand-off tour had only gotten to "this is the trailer" before the guy who was supposed to train him got a call from his wife and took off.  It turns out it was surrounded by, well, the fence and many shipping containers. He could navigate by the fence, since his driver from the airport had dropped him off at the--

"South Gate" he said, pointing at the chained gate. It did indeed look like it had been an entry years ago. The whole place looked like it might have been something years ago. He turned completely about.  "You are north" he said to another flame as it waved.  He waved back. "There are mountains to the north. You are a mountain fire."

Harshal had been in the Los Angeles of the California from the United States of the Americas for less than five hours now. Three of those had been in the line at Department of Homeland Security. There were were many, many people in uniform. Those people did completely predictable people-in-uniform things. The stood about and sternly checked paperwork. Just like the military-base varietal of people-in-uniform people back home.  Stern paperwork faces atop slouched, bored postures.

He forced his own face to go stern, hopefully in way to match his uniform. Bad posture was easy for him, since he had been born crippled.

"Let's get to checking things out, make the Lady happy."  Presumably, since there is a gate to the South, the mall should be to the north. He started navigating the shipping container maze. Only after breaching the far side of their cluster did he realize he could have run along the tops more easily. New world.

Both he and wind paused at the exact same moment, as if they were both caught in awe by the enormous, misshapen bulk of the building in front of him. Its wandering girth obeyed no logic, growing along the pavement as if seeking sustenance from the asphalt surface. This was the Westfield Los Kowloones Mall, at 517 Los Kowloones Boulevard. Yes, that one. The initial structure had been built 65 years ago, some time in the 1980s. 517 had humble beginnings as an outdoor promenade, bathing consumers in the Southern California Sunshine. A few years later it was engulfed by suburban wave of teen-movie-driven-teen-movie-cosplay-teenagers. This gave 517 the soil it needed to grow a glass enclosure, air conditioning, a food court, an ice rink.

For two decades it had thrived. Growing up (a second then third story up the hill), out (patios became food courts), down (subterranean parking), sideways (satellite Outback Steakhouse and Tire Pros) and had even sent a tendril over Low Kowloones Boulevard to a sumptuous parking structure. It became the glory of malls across the land, the reigning king of Climate Controlled Commerce. People wrote songs in its honor. People filmed movies (movies!) in its shade. Hundreds of thousands of wallets drip-irrigated its cash registers. It bloomed every conceivable form of modern consumerism. 

They say that within its confines may yet reside a Hot Dog On A Stick.

Harsh was standing at the foot of royalty. This was the iconography of America. This was what was described around the campfires of his desert home. This is the landscape he had wondered at over the shoulders of homesick soldiers. 

He moved slowly. How to approach such a beast? They had both lived decades in the service of others, vehicles for transactions that meant nothing to either of them. The soul of the building was silent, but present.  His throat was dry. Harsh placed his hand on his heart. He made a shallow bow to one of the massive columns that reached out in greeting.

"Hello," he began.  "I..." He stopped. Four measures of a tranquil waltz. "I am here to take care of you." The building, the wind and the newly minted caretaker shared a silent moment. The wind was the first one to remember her duties.

It was impossible for him to identify all the sounds. He had never been in a city before. The wind had an entire orchestra of options for noises. Trailer Door on snare. Fence on timpani. An unstable dumpster provided and unstable middle brass. A fortunate position of a discarded pipe produced a clear, simple sine wave around C3.

The walls of 517 sounded the strings. More like a fiddle than a violin. More like something you'd hear while telling stories than sipping wine.

The promised "I will call you back" called back. He grabbed the phone quickly. The wind tugged his jacket, reminding him to announce his the title and name.

"Harshal." The woman had a voice like cello with no resin on the bow and came out like stings in a horror movie. "Are you on the roof? How is the roof?"
