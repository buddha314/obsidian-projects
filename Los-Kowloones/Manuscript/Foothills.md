Seven years later.

Harhal was alone for the first time in over thirty hours. It was 1 AM and he was on the shore of a vast asphalt lake that served as the Los Kowloones Mall parking lot. A fence larger than he'd ever seen enclosed him in a mishegoss of plywood, black tarp and chainlink. The fence disappeared into the night in two directions.  A chaotic wind was causing the fence to thump like irregular beats of high schooler tuning a timpani. The thin door to the guard trailer offered a weak companion snare. Opening, hesitating, smashing, opening again and looking around to see what the other doors were doing.

The gusts took the breath from your mouth like a gasp of terror. He smelled a new kind of desert on the wind. He sensed a vast city hidden behind the tall fences. He smelled a tremendous fire.

The urgency in his body echoed in the landscape about him. Sirens blared from indistinguishable directions, their panic cutting the wind. Volcanic plumes of fire spewed off "that way". He had no idea how far he was from the flames. He had no idea what had started the conflagration. He no idea what he was supposed to do. He was out of ideas.

Moments after being issued his "SECURITY" jacket, his phone and his gun by a man who quickly disappeared into the night, a woman had phoned to tell him someone had broken into the building. The wind prevented using drones and the fire made it an emergency.

"You are the only person at the site, since that bastard Ozcar is gone," the whisky voice said. He liked that voice, it was like an American blues singer. "With the fire coming, it's the right thing to do." She hesitated. "Also, we don't want the lawsuit. The water has been shut off, so the sprinklers won't go."

"Yes ma'am." He looked at the caller ID: Stevie Pancoatl, Chief Legal Counsel. Jordan Simons Private Equity.  None of this made sense to him.

"The cameras have been out for months, so I can't tell if you are moving your ass out of that trailer right now or night. But it sure as fuck sounds like you are not!" Stevie had a passionate affect and a Mexican accent. He knew she was Mexican because he knew a lot of Mexican-American soldiers back home.

Harshal had been in the Los Angeles of the California from the United States of the Americas for less than five hours now. Three of those had been in the line at Department of Homeland Security. There were were many, many people in uniform. Those people did completely predictable people-in-uniform things. The stood about and sternly checked paperwork. Just like the military-base varietal of people-in-uniform people back home.  Stern paperwork faces atop slouched, bored postures.

He forced his own face to go stern as he donned the jacket, hopefully in a way to match his new job, his new life in America. Bad posture was easy for him, since he had been born crippled.

"Where do I go? How do I get in?" Harshal was deciding if he should lock the guard shack.

"Call me back when you get in. Let me know what's going on. If the building catches on fire, call the Fire Department. All you have to do is use the app on your company phone. The alarms won't work, they've been offline for years." The tension rose in Stevie's voice.

"Then how did you know someone had-" The line went dead. He decided to leave the door unlocked and ran outside.

The trailer was near the corner of the property and the hand-off tour had only gotten to "this is the trailer" before the guy who was supposed to be training him got a call from home and took off.  The trailer was surrounded by fence and shipping containers. He could navigate by the fence, since his driver from the airport had dropped him off at the--

"South Gate" he said, pointing at the chained gate. It did indeed look like it had been an entry years ago. The whole place looked like it might have been something years ago. He turned completely about.  "You are north" he said to another flame as it waved.  He waved back. "There are mountains to the north. You are a mountain fire. I will treat you with respect and you will be kind to the people of Los Angeles."

Presumably, since there is a gate to the South, the mall should be to the north. He started navigating the shipping container maze. Only after breaching the far side of their cluster did he realize he could have run along the tops more easily. He would be able to bound along heights and ridges even here in this new world. He dashed north into the darkness, pushed by the wind towards a monstrous shadow.

Both he and wind paused at the exact same moment, as if they were both caught in awe by the enormous, misshapen bulk of the building. Its wandering girth obeyed no logic, growing haphazardly along the pavement as if seeking sustenance from the asphalt surface. This was the Westfield Los Kowloones Mall, at 517 Los Kowloones Boulevard. Yes, that one. The initial structure had been built 65 years ago, some time in the 1980s. 517 had humble beginnings as an outdoor promenade, bathing consumers in the Southern California Sunshine. A few years later it was engulfed by suburban wave of teen-movie-driven-teen-movie-cosplay-teenagers. This gave 517 the soil it needed to grow a glass enclosure, air conditioning, a food court, an ice rink.

For two decades it had thrived. Growing up (a second then third story up the hill), out (patios became food courts), down (subterranean parking), sideways (satellite Outback Steakhouse and Tire Pros) and had even sent a tendril over Low Kowloones Boulevard to a sumptuous parking structure. It became the glory of malls across the land, the Queen of Climate Controlled Commerce. People wrote songs in its honor. People filmed movies (movies!) in its caverns. Hundreds of thousands of wallets drip-irrigated the plentiful cash registers. It bloomed every conceivable form of modern consumerism. 

They say that within its confines may yet reside a Hot Dog On A Stick.

Harsh was standing at the foot of royalty. This was the iconography of America. This was what was described around the campfires of his desert home. This is the landscape he had wondered at over the shoulders of homesick soldiers. 

He moved slowly. How to approach such a beast? They had both lived decades in the service of others, vehicles for transactions that meant nothing to either of them. The soul of the building was silent, but present.  His throat was dry. Harsh placed his hand on his heart. He made a shallow bow to one of the massive columns that reached out in greeting.

"Hello," he began.  "I..." He stopped. Four measures of a tranquil waltz. "I am here to take care of you." The building, the wind and the newly minted caretaker shared a silent moment. The wind was the first one to remember her duties.

It was impossible for him to identify all the sounds. He had never been in a city before. The wind had an entire orchestra of options for noises. Trailer Door on snare. Fence on timpani. An unstable dumpster provided and unstable middle brass. A discarded pipe whistled in disharmony.

This was the majestic front of the building, He found the utility ladders on the east side and used a dumpster to reach the bottom rung. The safety cage increased his speed tremendously and he was on the roof of the lower section within a few seconds.

The property abutted a river to the east and a rail yard to the south. To the west were the ever-ascending towers of Los Angeles. The bulk of 517 extended up the hill to the north, exactly in the path of the descending fire.

The roof was covered in its own service structure. Harsh recognized solar panels, wind generators and could see the exhaust vents for a few food courts below. Just like all the army encampments that would rotate near the oil-rich desert mountains he had just left.

Where could the thief enter the building? He headed towards a cluster of skylights.  Sure enough, one of the panes had been shattered. Five figures in hoodies were doing an agitated dance over the breach.

Gun? No gun? He was an excellent shot, and even gave lessons to the seasonal infantrymen. That's how he learned English, Russian, a few forms of Chinese and some French. He knew he could draw if they got hostile, even with his weaker arm.  Flashlight, then.

"Hey! What are you doing here!" His voice was meaningless in the wind, but the light on the window whirled them all at once. American teenagers, not here to buy hot dogs.

Each raised one hand and pointed into the break with the other. Three of them used instinctively used a Dance Elective move to point their left foot out, toes up. A fourth considered it. The fifth spoke.

"Suchin fell in there! Do you have a rope or something? She looks hurt!" He was in panic. A flame erupted outside the fence.

"You kids get out of here. Go! Get on your phones, call your moms! I will get your friend." They all hesitated. "The fire is coming, if it gets here, you all die. I will get your friend. Go!" He tried to look stern, then decided to slap his hip to get their attention. They scrambled, each taking the reminder that they had phones and death was, in fact, scarier than parental scorn.

He leaned over the edge of the window. Exactly as advertised, a young woman was curled up below.  She was awake and groaning. The fall was high enough to be fatal, but had had the presence of mind to enlist some light fixtures in the journey. They lay beside her, not groaning.

Harsh sent a voice message to Stevie. "Boss, there is a woman trapped inside who is hurt. I am going to get her now."  The Fire Department App was on his home screen, he pressed it urgently.

"Welcome to Screaming Eagle Security. Please enter your username: "

H. A. E *backspace* R S. His thumbs were circling the screen like vipers.

"Do you need more time?" The input was cleared. "You may also provide your thumbprint."

He pressed his thumb hard to speed it up a bit. Hurt woman, remember? Stupid fucking app.

"Welcome to Screaming Eagle Security, Harshal. I see this is your first time using your phone. Let me help you get set up. This will only take a moment." The phone shattered the mall floor, never having learned if Harshal was more interested in sports, music or travel.

Okay okay okay okay okay. The solar panels were tarped. He grabbed those. The wind generator had a branch stuck in the blades. The turbine roared to life and the roof was flooded with lights. He and Suchin below covered their eyes quickly, but the governor freaked out at the high speeds and shut itself down. 

Catching the branch in the frame and wrapping the tarp about the stick, he rappelled down to the first set of lights, then collected the tarp for the second leg. A moment later, he was putting the tarp over Suchin.

"Where's Boon?" She asked, holding her head. Close up, she was older than the others. College age.

"I sent him home. Told him to call his parents.... Your parents?" Harsh had no children of his own, but he did love uncle-ing when he could.

"Yeah, he's my kid brother. FUCK! He wanted to see the fire. Such an idiot. I think my phone is broken." Harsh eyed the remains of his own.

"It's okay. Just stay calm. I'll get us out of here." Ashes began to rain around them. Neither of them wanted her to move, so he dragged her on the tarp into a Twenty-One Forever. He piled some tops old enough to come back in style on her to keep her warm. The nearby fire had already been doing that, but it's a sweet gesture.
