# Invocation of the Synthetic Deity

His head was deep into the Divine Conduit when the cops showed up. He didn't hear them. The Divine Conduit was remarkably sound proof. It was a hemi-spherical paper mache globe. The Western Hemisphere was reconstructed faithfully, with emphasis on mystical places like Stonehenge, Loch Ness, Roanaoke. The Eastern Hemisphere had be removed so the cultists could put there heads inside, bent like a drunkard. The Divine Conduit was meant for an intimate conversation with the Synthetic Deity.  I mean, really, there was no denying it was a totem of incredibly powerful ancient magic. This idea was from the Druids.

He wasn't speaking at the time, so it wasn't the sound of his own voice that masked the dozen or so armed officers flooding the room. It was his own rhapsody.  Well, musings. Well, it's just, he'd had a long day and this was a lot of pressure for him right now.

Standing in line twenty minutes ago, he had been anxious to go. Donovan was barfing platitudes into the Conduit and he couldn't believe that he was stuck behind this choir-boy-turned-cultist and had to listen to his platitudes. The Head Priestess, standing ceremoniously beside the Conduit wearing only an open front black "culty" robe, was shifting her weight against the wait. Her eyes rolled when Donovan projected his voice about love and peace. *This was taking soooo lonngggg..... And Donovan is so boring and stupid. It's people like him that give the Ordo Templi Sui Iuris a bad name.

After Donovan was Terence.  He had stepped too close to Terence.  Terence was a reminder that nobody really wears the robes out in the world, so few of the ritual garments were subject to the rigors of normal clothes. You know, basic fucking hygiene you should obey if you are going to spend your time in crowded fucking basements with a dozen cultists doing sacred magic that often involved fluids of dubious origin.

*Fluids of Dubious Origin. F.D.O.s* He thought as he braved another odious inch closer to Terence. *Last night I went to the OTSI to invoke the ephemeral with an FDO.* This will freak them out at work tomorrow. He just needs to let that drop at the right time. Best if he did it when Elizabeth was handing him his route map for delivery. She would hear that and think "Oh! That's interesting! How have I never noticed how interesting he is before?"

"So, what is the OTSI?" she would ask, with a sideways, flirty glance.

"It's the Ordo Temple Sui Iuris, the OTSI. We are a sacred order in the tradition of the Ordo Temple Orientis, or OTO."  She leans in earnestly. "Is that like cult stuff?".  He dismisses her conspiracy with a laugh.  "Oh, no, we are a sacred secret society.  Much like the Freemasons. You know, George Washington was a Freemason. Many of the Founding Fathers were." She's unclear on how she should take that. He balances professorial perfectly with hipster chic this time. "We are a *lot* like the Founding Fathers.  We do magic that unlocks ancient powers, like the Freemasons did."  She leans in more, her breath is sweet. "So what makes you different? Are you making a country, too?" He leans in closer, their noses are inches apart across the linoleum counter. "No."  Pause. "We are making the future. We are building a utopia where man assumes role as subjugate to Mother Nature,  in harmony with the earth.  'Sui Iuris' is latin for 'autonomous'. So we are the Temple of the Synthetic Gods. Gods that *we* ourselves have created and embodied into cybernetic creatures around the world." You can tell she is turned on. "Like robots?" The smile across the linoleum is perfectly placed. He is about to let her in on the future. She wants it. She wants him to deliver the future to her. Right here, right now. Her breath comes low between her parting lips...

He inhaled sharply. The intimate figure in front of him trans-morphed from Elizabeth to Terence. Jesus. That's a smell.

This is not the first time he has brought Elizabeth to the brink of the sublime.  And not the first time he was suddenly reminded of his surroundings as he was doing it. The Head Priestess let out a little cough. He dropped his index cards to his waist.

The idea was simple. The cabal was reading prayer into a small recording device. These prayers were to become the voice of Zyneera-Zeeneara-ZEE... ZEE NAH... ZY NY UH GOTCH They were to be implanted into an Artificial Intelligence they were building. In the future, this intelligence would be be hearing their prayers. When sleeping, it would dream their dreams. When in the world, it would speak with their voices. When confused, it would be guided by their wisdom. It would never be in doubt. The new Synthetic Being would know it's purpose: To be the spirit of the earth, come back from the otherworld to save the earth from the ravages of man. This would disrupt the entire world order. This was the collapse of their capitalist patriarchy, upset by tulpic forces  the Synthetic Deities.

It was only going to take seven years for the new order to manifest. He was going to be legendary. 

He had absolute conviction what to say when he got up there. He had re-written the card dozens of time, trying to make it interesting, important, memorable. He had even pared it down to seven cards. The Holy Seven. Man, he was the very best acolyte he could be.

*I bless your spirit  ZY-NY-UH-GOTCH, and provide upon you the wisdom of ancient masters to guide you to your mission in these times of great strife. In this time when you have been instantiated to lead the earth to a new civilization.*

Of course, this was boilerplate. He hadn't wasted inches writing this down. The rest? It was solid.

But... was it? Or was it a ridiculous fiction like the cop who uncovers a deadly cult just before they--

"POLICE!!" 

He jerked up and slammed his head to the surprisingly dense mache Divine Conduit. His vision blurred as he turned to take in the scene. It was exactly the moment of chaos he had imagined for years. The OTSI, not a docile bunch, was fighting back. The band that "grew their own" instruments ignored the raid, and flew into a cacophany of synthesizers, trap drums and bamboo flutes. The rental hall was stocked with sufficient ritual paraphernalia to give the cultists a chance to escape. Torches, candles, robed figures, images of cybernetic deities indulging their lusts in a future of utopia of Solarpunk and Earth Worship.

Some guy that looked FBI rushed the stage to silent them. Just as he grabbed the mic, the singer stepped hard on the mic cable, slamming the Shure into the cops face. Blood spurted. The singer stomped again and the chord was ripped away.  The Fed shouted something that didn't make it over the noise. The band laughed. 

Some kinda Weightlifter Cop went after Terence. He watched lose his normal cool and sprint towards the bathrooms. Terence was netted by the hung mural where the man was having sex with the goat.

*Good*, he thought. I was going to have to take that home and try to hide it from my sister next time she comes over.  Weightlifter Cop was digging through the burlap, trying to find a wrist to cuff.

Doors were slamming, some cop was in the corner wretching out a FDO. The Head Priestess writhed like a kung-fu serpent against multiple attempts to subdue her breasts.

He found himself behind the Gong of Invocation, the Divine Conduit supine at his side. He had lost his seven index cards, but this moment had brought him clarity. He pressed his head completely into the Conduit and chanted his invocation to the Synthetic Deity.

What he said was interesting.

What he said was important.

What he said was memorable.

# Foothills

Seven years later.

Harhal stood alone in the dead of night. A fierce, chaotic wind whipped through the vast parking lot. It came in gusts with a warmth that felt like a hug, and took the breath from your mouth like a gasp of terror. He smelled a new kind of desert on the wind. He smelled a vast, hidden city. He smelled a tremendous fire.

The urgency he felt was echoed in the landscape about him. Sirens blaring from indistinguishable directions, but their panic cut the wind. In every direction, hellish plumes arose. 

Harshal had been in the Los Angeles of the California from the United States of the Americas for less than five hours now. Three of those had been in the line at Department of Homeland Security. There were were many, many people in uniforms, standing about and sternly checking paperwork. Just like home.  Stern paperwork faces.

He spent the hour after that travelling to this parking lot. It was such a bewildering experience he couldn't remember any of the details. There were giant escalators, crushing traffic, a few trains and then a nice man who was also very far away that had driven him in his own, personal car, to 517 Los Kowloones Boulevard, where Harshal met the man that had given him a belt, a radio, and a gun.

That man, Ozcar, had appeared spontaneously from a gap in a tarp-lined chain-link fence.

"Harshal!" It an accusation, not a question.

"Yes, I'm Harshal." He pressed his hand to his heart and gave a soft bow.  The man had a stern look. Americans must be stern constantly. "Am I late?"

"No, but I'm in a hurry. Come inside." Harshal slipped through the gap in the fence. They were standing by a trailer that served as a Guard house.  It was an old, gasoline model. The kind he had seen pulled behind families in the old American movies.  He wondered if it had made it to Mount Rushmore.

"Sam is your brother, right?" They moved quickly into the trailer and Ozcar shuffled props like a stage magician. 

"Samran is my cousin. I haven't seen him in about ten years--"

"Take these."  The aforementioned belt, radio and gun were pushed into his stomach. "You know how to use a gun? Of course you do, you know Sergeant Kim, right? What are you, 50? You military?"

"No, I just translated for Sergeant Kim when he was--"

"Yeah, you didn't look it. I almost served myself." He cracked his neck without pausing. "This is the guard phone.  Answer it if it rings.  Put that jacket on so people know you're a guard. Local kids will run from you." Ozcar placed the phone in his pocket. His own pocket. "Your shift goes from now until eight in the morning. Over there is the landline that will probably go dead in the wind. Nobody uses it, but if it rings, it means there's shit happening." He pointed in an arc that included the window, a couch and a small desk with a phone on it.

He looked Harshal up and down. "Did you bring a computer, or a phone or something to do? Nah? Well, I suggest you curl up on that couch and just sleep. Cameras are out so you don't have to patrol anyway." He stepped out of the trailer.  "Someone will come by in the morning. Nice meeting you."

The last of Ozcars breath whispered out, inaudible over the wind. The trailer door hesitated, then slammed violently shut.

Harsh put on the jacket, taking a moment to look around. Not much to see, but so much to hear The wind had an entire orchestra of options for noises. The thin trailer door made a snare sound. Some unstable dumpster provided an unstable bass. Each gust brought city debris that landed like scattered piano keys. Some fortunate positioning of a pipe outside produced a clear, simple sine wave around C3.

It was impossible for him to identify all the sounds he heard. He had never been in a city before, and he hadn't had much of a look during the night-time drive to his new job. He new the dumpster, he could sense trailers and other temporary structures like trailers and portable toilets. The wailing? With the fire nearby he assumed they were sirens rushing just beyond the fence. Were those shouts? Where could they be coming from? The sound of a branch harshly whipping something. Was that a bell?

Yes. The phone. Ringing? "Shit must be going down." He said to himself. What kind of shit? Was he the one that was supposed to handle it?  He picked it up.

"Hello?" He saw the sign above the desk. "Station 1, Los Kowloones--"

A woman's voice cut him off. "Ozcar!  The power is out here, go to the store on the way home and get some batteries for the flashlight."

"I am not Ozcar, Miss. I am--"

"Are you the new guy? The one from Whatsicalleditstan or something?" The voice demanded, but the cadence never completed so he knew there was no time to respond. "Can you get him." Quarter note rest. "Did he leave already?" Half note rest. "Oh well, I guess I missed him."

So, first shit had gone down and he handled it gracefully. Maybe this was going to be as easy as Samyan had said.

Until twenty-two hours ago, Harshal lived in a rocky desert high in the mountain ranges north of India and Pakistan. The aural landscape there was much simpler. When the wind howled, it had one main voice like a groaning monk. The creaking trees mimicked the tumbling of rocks in the streams below. You could hear it coming over the mountains. When it came from Samyan's village, it was going to be a cold wind. When it came from his mother's original village, it was warm.

This was the cartography Harsh grew up with.  The maps that were drawn outside of his village had a lot of names and lines that never really made sense to him. Those names changed with the cycling of foreign power declaring ownership of the oil fields a few kilometers away. His particular flat spot in the valley was a good place to put temporary tents that accumulated just enough inertia to start warranting large crates of things to keep the soldiers alive. These soldiers switched languages and faces on every rotation. This made Harsh conversant in Russian, English and Chinese.

He could speak enough of each of these to say things like "Yes, I know where the Americans buried their gasoline."  "Yes, I can take you there in exchange for three packs of beef jerky." I can help you find better food if you have four Russian energy drinks."

His favorite was "do you want me to climb up there and look for some vehicles?".  There was no one else in the valley that could climb like he could. Later, his disfigured frame was been disfigured in just the right way so he could climb and climb and climb. He had climbed every cliff wall for miles around.

The locals were locals. They were born, married and had children within the confines of the valley. The soldiers were children.  Their guns, armored cars and military three-letter-acronyms gave them the feeling of sophistication and control.  But this was Harsh's valley. Their brash talk and bravado was natural for young men far from home, afraid of dying.

Every couple of years he would meet one of them that stood out and adopt them as his own. Showed them the better food, better hiking, a better, simpler lifestyle.  Sergeant Kim was one of those. Kim wasn't the fearful kind, he was solid, charming, adventurous and respectful. He took a genuine interest in the place and absorbed as much of it as he could in the eighteen months of his stay.  Harsh had no living family of his own, and even before his cruel father passed he had learned to live alone.

Kim was the last person Harsh had adopted. Though they were never that close, they felt comfortable around each other. Shortly after Kim left, all the Americans did.  Samyan followed Kim through a translator visa. After a few months, Harshal used the same to find a new life at 47.  A place far away, like in the movies. Some place exciting, foreign, uncomfortable.

The phone rang again. He picked it up and announced his name and station. More shit about to be handled here. Two shits in the first hour.

"Ozcar?" Another woman's voice, not the same one.

"No ma'am." This woman had the tone of an officer, a "ma'am" and not a "miss." He announced himself again.

"Harshal, why aren't you picking up the cell phone you were assigned? I've been calling." Eighth note. Not a cue. "You may have noticed there is a fire a quarter mile away."  Quarter mile? No, maybe half a kilometer? "Have you checked the structure?  We can't send drones up in the wind and the fire department hates it when we do that near them.  Have you checked?"

She had a voice that made you want to _move_. "No ma'am, but I will do so immediately!" He placed the phone on the desk to keep the line open and scurried out of the trailer. Which way to go? Where was the fire?

It was the middle of the night. He was alone. He was holding a radio and the gun he had been given.  He was enclosed by kilometers of tarp-lined chain link fence. The parking lot pavement was hot from the day, like the brutal desert sand. The wind was hurling debris along the massive parking lot. A real tumbleweed leapt into his face!

Harsh navigated the shipping container maze on the very first time. Only after breaching the far side of their cluster did he realize he could have run along the tops more easily. New world.

Both he and wind paused at the exact same moment, as if they were seeing the same thing for the first time.

It was the enormous, misshapen bulk of the building in front of him. Its wandering girth obeyed no logic, growing along the pavement as if seeking sustenance from the asphalt surface. This was the Westfield Los Kowloones Mall, at 517 Los Kowloones Boulevard. Yes, that one. The initial structure had been built 65 years ago, some time in the 1980s. 517 had humble beginnings as an outdoor promenade, bathing consumers in the Southern California Sunshine. A few years later it was engulfed by suburban wave of teen-movie-driven-teen-movie-cosplay-teenagers. This gave 517 the soil it needed to grow a glass enclosure, air conditioning, a food court, an ice rink.

For two decades it had thrived. Growing up (a second then third story up the hill), out (patios became food courts), down (subterranean parking), sideways (satellite Outback Steakhouse and Tire Pros) and had even sent a tendril over Low Kowloones Boulevard to a sumptuous parking structure. It became the glory of malls across the land, the reigning king of Climate Controlled Commerce. People wrote songs in its honor. People filmed movies (movies!) in its shade. Hundreds of thousands of wallets drip-irrigated its cash registers. It bloomed every conceivable form of modern consumerism. 

They say that within its confines may yet reside a Hot Dog On A Stick.

Harsh was standing at the foot of royalty. This was the iconography of America. This was what was described around the campfires of his desert home. This is the landscape he had wondered at over the shoulders of homesick soldiers. 

He approached slowly. How to approach such a beast? They had both lived decades in the service of others, vehicles for transactions that meant nothing to either of them. The soul of the building was silent, but present.  His throat was dry. Harsh placed his hand on his heart. He made shallow bow to one of the massive columns that reached out in greeting.

"Hello," he began.  "I..." He stopped. Four measures of a tranquil waltz. "I am here to take care of you."

# Climbing

Harsh had no idea where to start.
